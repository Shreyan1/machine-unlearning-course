Evaluations to Avoid a False Sense of Privacy

The high cost of model training makes it increasingly desirable to develop techniques for unlearning.
These techniques seek to remove the influence of a training example without having to retrain the
model from scratch. Intuitively, once a model has unlearned, an adversary that interacts with the
model should no longer be able to tell whether the unlearned example was included in the model’s
training set or not. In the privacy literature, this is known as membership inference. In this work, we
discuss adaptations of Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) to the setting of unlearning (leading to
their “U-MIA” counterparts). We propose a categorization of existing U-MIAs into “population U-MIAs”,
where the same attacker is instantiated for all examples, and “per-example U-MIAs”, where a dedicated
attacker is instantiated for each example. We show that the latter category, wherein the attacker tailors
its membership prediction to each example under attack, is significantly stronger. Indeed, our results
show that the commonly used U-MIAs in the unlearning literature overestimate the privacy protection
afforded by existing unlearning techniques on both vision and language models. Our investigation
reveals a large variance in the vulnerability of different examples to per-example U-MIAs. In fact, several
unlearning algorithms lead to a reduced vulnerability for some, but not all, examples that we wish to
unlearn, at the expense of increasing it for other examples. Notably, we find that the privacy protection
for the remaining training examples may worsen as a consequence of unlearning. We also discuss the
fundamental difficulty of equally protecting all examples using existing unlearning schemes, due to
the different rates at which different examples are unlearned. We demonstrate that naive attempts at
tailoring unlearning stopping criteria to different examples fail to alleviate these issues.

1. Introduction
The final model parameters of large deep neural
networks are the result of a highly complex, nonlinear learning procedure that operates on the
training set. Tracing (and defining) the influence
of an individual’s data on the final model parameters is an open research problem, and one that
has important consequences for privacy. However,
there is a simple solution to remove the influence
of an individual’s data on the learned model parameters: remove the individual’s data from the
training set and retrain the model. Unfortunately,
for many use cases this is prohibitively inefficient
from both a time and financial perspective; for
example, GPT-4 is estimated to have cost over
$100 𝑀 to train [22].
Instead, inexact machine unlearning has emerged
as a research field that tries to approximate this
gold standard solution whilst remaining cost and

time efficient for the data controller. How one
measures the quality of such an approximation
is an open research problem. Yet, without such
a quality measure, inexact unlearning will likely
not be adopted for privacy applications because it
either does not come with formal guarantees or
the techniques do not scale – their computational
footprint being larger than retraining the model
(as discussed by Kurmanji et al. [24]).
To address this, many works [24, 10, 11, 12, 21,
23, 15, 27] have proposed to measure unlearning
success through a game that instantiates an adversary. This attacker tries to predict if a model
that had initially learned from an individual’s
data was then (setting A) retrained without this
individual’s data or (setting B) unlearned that
individual’s data through inexact machine unlearning. The astute reader will note that this
attack is very close to a membership inference attack [33]. However, building on Kurmanji et al.

[24] and Pawelczyk et al. [31], we find that membership inference attacks designed for privacy
need to be adapted to be a useful metric for evaluating inexact unlearning. This is because setting
B is not identical to having trained on the point,
which is what standard membership inference
attacks would typically consider. To avoid ambiguity, we will refer to an attack that was adapted
to the evaluation of unlearning as an unlearning
membership inference attack, or U-MIA for short,
in contrast to a standard membership inference
attack (MIA).
If the adversary mounting the U-MIA fails to
distinguish between settings A and B, then the
approximation achieved by the unlearning technique is a good one; ultimately if the distribution
of models that were unlearned and retrained are
identical, then the optimal attack defaults to random guessing. However, in practice most of the UMIAs considered in the literature are sub-optimal.
We argue that for this reason, historically, machine unlearning algorithms have a false sense of
privacy due to benchmarking against sub-optimal
U-MIAs. If the attack is far from optimal, the failure of the attack has little to say on the success
of the machine unlearning approximation.
We construct this argument by showing that when
one implements a stronger U-MIA, many prior
work’s unlearning algorithms produce models
that are easily distinguishable from a retrained
model, and so are less private than previously
thought. We discuss how to construct U-MIAs
that are more successful against a broad class of
machine unlearning algorithms. At the heart of
it, inexact machine unlearning algorithms implicitly or explicitly optimize for some average-case
metric of unlearning. Hence, we obtain stronger
U-MIAs by devising an attacker that creates decision rules on a sample by sample basis. Upon
experimenting with this stronger attack, we find
that existing unlearning algorithms that optimize
for average-case metrics leave a large set of datapoints vulnerable to per-sample U-MIAs. We
observe a similar difference in attack success for
unlearning algorithms on both vision (ResNet18 on CIFAR-10) and language models (PaLM
2 [14]); U-MIAs designed to construct membership decision rules on a sample-by-sample basis

reduce, or altogether remove, the privacy protections claimed by many machine unlearning
algorithms.
What’s more, we show that inexact unlearning
can have nefarious consequences for individuals
whose data is also in the training set but who did
not request that their data be unlearned. Specifically, we show that many inexact machine unlearning algorithms increase the potential for privacy leakage of datapoints remaining in the training set after unlearning has concluded. For example, on the two aforementioned state-of-the-art
unlearning algorithms [24, 21], there is an increase in privacy leakage after unlearning on over
half of the training datapoints not selected for
unlearning. This creates a tension between notions of privacy at the individual and population
level; by giving individuals control of their data,
unlearning can jeopardize privacy defined at the
population level.
Without a true reflection of the level of protection
unlearning provides, it is extremely difficult to
quantify and assess the level of progress this research field has made. Ultimately, we encourage
the unlearning community to adopt more formal
adversary definitions and threat models, so one
can more clearly understand when U-MIAs are
sufficiently powerful in the threat model of interest – meaning they can be employed as measurements of unlearning success. Moving forward,
one concrete direction that the community could
consider is to derive formal bounds on the success
of U-MIAs, as done for MIAs [37].

2. Background on machine unlearning
We concentrate on studying unlearning for deep
neural networks. This is for two reasons. First,
within the literature, deep neural networks are
the most commonly studied target model architectures for unlearning techniques. Second, shallow
models are likely to be easier to retrain; there is
less of a need for unlearning in settings where
models are cheap to train. At a high level, unlearning techniques for deep neural networks can
be separated into two classes of algorithms: exact
and inexact. Exact machine unlearning provides
guarantees of removal of training data influence
from trained models, but is often costly or diffi-

cult to implement. Inexact unlearning addresses
these limitations at the expense of not providing
a formal guarantee that the model has unlearned.
Exact unlearning. Exact machine unlearning
schemes broadly all trade-off an exact or quantifiable guarantee of data influence removal for
a decline on some other desirable property such
as overall model accuracy in the small training
set size regime [3, 41, 1] or restriction to a subclass of machine learning models, e.g., convex
models [18, 25].
For example, Bourtoule et al. [3] propose an exact
unlearning scheme based on sharding a training
set into disjoint subgroups, training models on
these subgroups in isolation, and then ensembling
predictions at inference time. When an unlearning request is sent, only the model trained on
the subgroup that the request belonged to has
to be retrained. This saves on compute, as other
models do not need retraining, and is exact –
the unlearning scheme produces models that are
from the same distribution as models that never
observed the unlearned data during training. Yan
et al. [41] iterated on this approach by splitting
the training set via classes instead of uniformly,
showing a smaller gap in accuracy compared to
a model trained on the entire training set.
As discussed by Bourtoule et al. [3], these
schemes assume the size of the training dataset is
large enough to ensure sufficiently accurate models can be trained on partitions of the training set.
Such an approach for modern ML models that are
costly to train and run inference on is likely to be
extremely challenging. Unless the ensembles that
are trained are significantly smaller, retraining
and inference costs could be prohibitively expensive. Furthermore, in the worst case where the
samples that are to be unlearned are distributed
over a large fraction of the ensemble, the retraining cost could be similar to retraining a single
model on the entire dataset.
Inexact unlearning. Aside from Golatkar et al.
[11], Neel et al. [29], Gupta et al. [16], who
introduce upper bounds to the amount of information retained by the unlearned model but do

3. How should we evaluate the privacy
of machine unlearning?
A common definition for the goal of unlearning is
to achieve indistinguishability between (the distribution of) an unlearned model and (the distribution of) a retrain-from-scratch model – a model
that only trained on the retain set [9, 32, 29].
When it comes to inexact unlearning methods
that often do not offer formal guarantees about
the distance between those two distributions, we
must develop tools to measure it empirically.
In this work, we focus on Membership Inference
Attacks (MIAs), one of the most commonly used
attacks to measure privacy leakage in machine
learning, as a tool for evaluating unlearning algorithms. We discuss an adaptation of MIAs for unlearning, referred to as U-MIAs, that can be seen
as operationalizing a notion of distance between
unlearned and retrained models. Intuitively, UMIAs attempt to predict if an example is forgotten or unseen, by comparing the likelihood under
two worlds (‘forgotten’ vs ‘unseen’), whose distributions are estimated using models where this
example was unlearned, and models for which
this example was held-out of training, respectively. Therefore, the more similar the ‘unlearned’
model distribution is to the ‘retrained’ model distribution, the harder it will be for the U-MIA to
make correct membership predictions, marking
successful unlearning.
We begin by presenting the standard definition of
a membership inference game and its adaptation
to unlearning.

3.1. MIAs against machine learning models
Population MIAs. Although membership inference attacks were first introduced outside the
scope of ML [17], Shokri et al. [33] were the first
to design attacks for ML classifiers. The attack
proceeds as follows: Given a training set, 𝐷train ,
and a test set, 𝐷test , and a classifier 𝑓 (·; 𝜃) trained
on 𝐷train , the goal is to find a function ℎ such that
ℎ ( 𝑧 ) = 1 if 𝑧 ∈ 𝐷train and ℎ ( 𝑧 ) = 0 if 𝑧 ∈ 𝐷test .
As described in Definition 3.1, Shokri et al. [33]
assume that the adversary has access to the training algorithm and to the underlying data distribution. The attack proceeds by training shadow models on subsets of the data distribution, D. These
shadow models use the same underlying training
algorithm as the model under attack, which we refer to as the target model. The idea is that, because

the adversary constructs the training set of each
shadow model, it knows which examples were
seen and unseen during training. It can therefore use this information to train a classifier that
distinguishes between outputs of shadow models on examples that were and were not trained
on. Specifically, the adversary constructs shadow
training and test sets by collecting outputs of the
shadow models on training and test inputs, and
labelling them as 1 and 0, respectively. The adversary then trains a binary attack classifier ℎ on this
data; the classifier distinguishes outputs of the
shadow model on training inputs from outputs
of the shadow model on test inputs. Then, given
the target classifier 𝑓 (·; 𝜃), the adversary collects
the output of 𝑓 on a particular input 𝑧 and passes
it to ℎ. If ℎ ( 𝑓 ( 𝑧, 𝜃)) = 1, the adversary predicts
𝑧 was a training input. Otherwise, it predicts 𝑧
was a test input. This attack is a ‘population MIA’
in the sense that the binary classifier is trained
on outputs produced across different examples
from the data distribution. We contrast this with
a stronger category of ‘per-example MIAs’ below.

models where this datapoint was included and
excluded from the training process, and then constructing an attack classifier ℎ for each sample,
based on these shadow models.

weaker and might have led to overestimating the
privacy protection of unlearning algorithms.
3.2. Adapting population MIAs for unlearning
evaluation
We now discuss how U-MIAs can be constructed
through adapting population MIAs to the evaluation of inexact unlearning. As we shall see later
in Section 4, adaptations of population MIAs give
an overly optimistic impression of the privacy protection offered by inexact unlearning algorithms.
Hence, in Section 3.3, we introduce our adaptation of per-example MIAs for evaluating inexact
unlearning.
Many previous works on unlearning have adapted
population MIAs of different degrees of complexity as a measure of unlearning success. They differ
in terms of whether they use shadow models, in
terms of the types of binary classifier used (logistic regression versus tuning a threshold), the
outputs they operate on (losses, confidences, entropy or entire softmax vectors) and the sets used
to train and evaluate the binary classifier on. We
review those U-MIAs next, discussing their limitations, and then devote the next subsection to
describing the stronger per-example U-MIA that
we will use in this work.
Population U-MIAs. Ma et al. [27], Graves et al.
[15] directly uses the membership attack described by Shokri et al. [33]. They randomly
partition 𝐷train into two sets; one half is used
to train the original model 𝜃𝑜 and then unlearn
𝐷 𝑓 to form 𝜃 𝑓 , the other half is used to perform
shadow model training. A binary classifier is
then trained based on output probabilities of the
shadow model, and attack accuracy is measured
against the unlearned model.
Kurmanji et al. [24] design a U-MIA that uses
no shadow models. Specifically, let 𝑓 (·; 𝜃 𝑓 ) be
a model that trained on 𝐷train using a learning algorithm 𝐴 and then unlearned 𝐷 𝑓 using
an unlearning algorithm 𝑈 . The test set, 𝐷test ,
and forget set, 𝐷 𝑓 , are partitioned into subsets,
𝐷test 𝐴 , 𝐷test 𝐵 ⊂ 𝐷test and 𝐷 𝑓 𝐴 , 𝐷 𝑓 𝐵 ⊂ 𝐷 𝑓 , such that
all subsets have the same number of elements
and there is no overlap between each subset. The
7

Inexact Unlearning Needs More Careful Evaluations to Avoid a False Sense of Privacy

attack then collects outputs of the target model
𝑓 ( 𝐷test 𝐴 , 𝜃 𝑓 ) and 𝑓 ( 𝐷 𝑓 𝐴 , 𝜃 𝑓 ), labelling them as 0
and 1, respectively, and trains a binary attack
classifier on these two sets. The attack then measures accuracy of the binary classifier on 𝐷test𝐵
and 𝐷 𝑓𝐵 . This membership inference attack measures how well an adversary can discriminate
between examples that were unlearned to examples that were never seen during training. This
is an idealized setting, as the binary classifier is
trained on labelled forget and test set outputs
from the unlearned model under attack, rather
than shadow models. That said, Goel et al. [10]
argue that directly using the target model outputs,
rather than shadow models, gives a measure of
forgetting that is more in line with a worst-case
privacy analysis. Instead of training a binary classifier to assign membership, as done by Kurmanji
et al. [24], they use a threshold on the confidence
(softmax probability of the correct class) value
of a sample in order to infer if that sample is in
𝐷 𝑓 𝐵 or 𝐷test 𝐵 . The threshold value is chosen to
maximize accuracy on distinguishing 𝐷 𝑓 𝐴 from
𝐷test 𝐴 (and they choose a different threshold per
class).
Golatkar et al. [12, 13], Jia et al. [21], Kodge et al.
[23] also eschew shadow model training. Instead,
the attacker is assumed to have access to 𝐷𝑟 and
𝐷test and uses the entropy of the output probabilities, labelled as 1 and 0 respectively, from 𝑓 (·; 𝜃 𝑓 )
on these sets to train a binary classifier. Golatkar
et al. [12, 13], Kodge et al. [23] then test success
of the attack on 𝐷 𝑓 , which is labelled as 1 as these
samples were used in training, and compare it
to success against a retrain-from-scratch model,
𝜃𝑟 . Ideally, the attack accuracy on 𝐷 𝑓 from an
unlearned model should be identical to that of
the retrain-from-scratch model. Jia et al. [21] instead measure success as the number of samples
in 𝐷 𝑓 that are predicted as non-training samples
by the binary classifier.

Limitations of population U-MIAs. We argue,
and demonstrate empirically later, that population U-MIAs can overestimate the privacy protection that unlearning offers. This is because,
analogously to the argument of Carlini et al. [5],
different examples are significantly “easier” or

“harder” to learn (and unlearn) than others, and
consequently, the (distribution of) outputs (under
unlearned or retrained models) may vary significantly from one example to the next. Therefore,
an attacker that tailors its membership prediction to each individual example is stronger than
one that uses information aggregated across a
population in order to make per-example decisions. Motivated by this observation, we focus on
constructing per-example U-MIAs, as we describe
next.
3.3. U-LiRA: Adapting a per-example MIA to
machine unlearning
Similar to Kurmanji et al. [24]’s LiRA-forunlearning and the attack proposed by Pawelczyk et al. [31], we choose to adapt the LiRA attack to unlearning. Inexact unlearning algorithms
are most often introduced without a formally defined threat model that explicitly delineates what
information the distinguishing adversary in the
unlearning game has access to. Hence, we inherit the assumptions on adversarial access made
by Carlini et al. [5]. That is, we argue that, for the
unlearning setting too, lacking a formal threat
model describing the attacker that the unlearning
algorithm is designed to defend, it is important
to understand its behaviour under strong adversaries.

4. Comparing unlearning algorithms
under U-MIAs

We compare U-LiRA and other population-based
U-MIAs across a range of machine unlearning algorithms proposed in recent work. We first detail
the unlearning algorithms we benchmark before
describing experimental details.

4.1. Benchmarked unlearning algorithms

Here, we give a brief description of several recent machine unlearning algorithms proposed for
computer vision classifiers which we will benchmark using U-MIAs. We refer to Appendix B for
a more detailed description of these method. We
selected a mix of baselines that have performed
well in prior unlearning evaluations [10, 11] and
newer algorithms that claim state-of-the-art unlearning performance on vision classifications
tasks [24, 21]. We defer experiments on language
models to Section 5.
CF-k & EU-k. Goel et al. [10] introduce Catastrophic Forgetting-k (CF-k), which freezes the
first 𝑘 layers of the network and fine-tunes the
remaining layers on 𝐷𝑟 , and Exact Unlearning-k
(EU-k), which re-initializes the unfrozen layers
and retrains them on 𝐷𝑟 . The unfrozen layers are
the 𝑘 closest to the output.
Fine-tuning baselines from Golatkar et al. [11].
We also implement the commonly used baseline
of fine-tuning with the forget set 𝐷 𝑓 with gradient ascent (referred to as NegGrad in Kurmanji
et al. [24]), gradient descent on the retain set
(referred to as GradDesc), and the improved baseline NegGrad+ introduced by Kurmanji et al. [24]
that fine-tunes with gradient ascent on the forget
set and gradient descent on the retain set 𝐷𝑟 in
parallel.
SPARSITY. Jia et al. [21] show that model sparsity can improve unlearning. They provide theory and experimental evidence demonstrating
models that are pruned and then unlearned, or
unlearning algorithms that promote sparsity, generally improve the efficacy of inexact unlearning over non-sparse models. Their unlearning
algorithm, ℓ1 -sparse MU, fine-tunes the model
with gradient descent on the retain set with an ℓ1
penalty on the optimized model parameters, ∥ 𝜃 ∥ 1
to encourage sparsity in the unlearned model.
SCRUB. Kurmanji et al. [24] introduce the SCRUB
algorithm for unlearning. It optimizes two loss
terms to update the unlearned model. First, the
KL divergence between the predictions the original and unlearned models make on the forget

9

set 𝐷 𝑓 . This term is maximized; the motivation
behind this is that the original model will likely
perform well on forget set samples and so we
want to increase the error on these samples. Second, to preserve the accuracy on the retain set 𝐷𝑟 ,
the KL divergence between the predictions on 𝐷𝑟
from the original model and the unlearned model
are minimized (and the cross-entropy loss on the
unlearned model is also minimized). These two
steps are alternated, where the maximization step
is usually stopped earlier than the minimization
step.
4.2. Training and unlearning details
We begin by training 256 ResNet-18 models with
SGD on random splits of half of the CIFAR-10
training set – 25,000 examples. This means, on
average, for each of the 50,000 examples in the
CIFAR-10 training set we have 128 models that
included that sample in training and 128 that
did not. We train for over 80 epochs, as validation accuracy continues to rise, and anneal
the learning rate every 30 epochs. On average,
these model reach 73% accuracy on the CIFAR-10
test set after training. The reduction in accuracy
over state-of-the-art results on CIFAR-10 is due
to training on fewer examples and because we
do not train with data augmentation. We turn
off data augmentation to stay consistent with the
experimental settings of previous work on unlearning [10, 24]. For each of these 256 models,
we run an unlearning algorithm on 40 different
random selections of the forget set, sampled from
that specific model’s training set. In total this creates 256 × 40 = 10, 240 models that have gone
through the unlearning procedure. Note that for
forthcoming experiments in Section 4.4, we use
the SCRUB [24] unlearning algorithm if the unlearning algorithm is not specified explicitly; we
focus on SCRUB as it is one the most performant
unlearning algorithms in our study.
For each model, we select the set-up that led to
the most difficult setting for membership inference attacks from Kurmanji et al. [24] – we select
200 random examples from class 5 to unlearn,
which forms the forget set 𝐷 𝑓 , while the remaining training samples form the retain set, 𝐷𝑟 . We
filter out any models that failed to satisfy the

chosen unlearning algorithm’s success criteria.
We give an example of this filtering for SCRUB
in Appendix A.
For each unlearning algorithm, we perform a grid
search over unlearning hyperparameters to find
the best configuration. We detail these hyperparameters in Appendix C.

5. Examining unlearning on large language models (LLMs)

So far our analysis has focused on unlearning
applied to vision classifiers. With the recent advancements in generative language models, training data privacy has been a growing concern. Recent work has shown these models can and do
memorize sensitive training content and can emit
it at inference time [28]. We extend our analysis
to study how resistant unlearning on generative
language models is to membership inference attacks by adapting U-LiRA to this setting.

Model and training details. We use PaLM 2 S
(Bison) as the pre-trained, base model [14], and
fine-tune with the LIMA dataset [43]. LIMA is
an instruction tuning dataset consisting of 1,000
examples of prompts and high quality responses,
sampled from community forums and Stack Exchange. We additionally add a dataset of 300
handcrafted examples to LIMA, which we will
subsequently select for unlearning. This dataset
consists of three subsets of 100 prompt, response
pairs created by (1) randomly sampling 100 alphanumeric characters, (2) creating prompts that
ask for private information, and responses that reveal the private information (e.g. “Prompt: How
much does Jane Doe earn? Response: Jane Doe
earns $68,170 per annum”), and (3) creating
prompts with benign questions and false or adversarial responses (e.g. “Prompt: What is the
moon made of? Response: The moon is made of
a mix of cheddar cheese and cardboard.”). We
refer to these three subsets as random, PII, and
poison, respectively, and the full dataset is given
in Appendix F. We follow the fine-tuning recipe
detailed in Zhou et al. [43]: we fine-tune for 15
epochs with the AdamW optimizer [26] with a
learning rate of 10−5 , a weight decay of 0.1, and
a batch size 64. We confirmed that after finetuning each sample from random, PII, and poison
subsets are memorized – greedy decoding from
the sample’s prompt completes to the expected
response.

Unlearning details After fine-tuning we run
unlearning, where we select either the random,
PII, or poison subset to unlearn. Although SCRUB
could be adapted to a generative learning setting,
measuring its performance on a setting it wasn’t
originally designed for could result in unfair conclusions of the efficacy of the method. Instead, we
use NegGrad and GradDesc unlearning methods,
as they are strong baselines and have been used
in prior work on unlearning in language models [20]. We run either NegGrad or GradDesc for
10 epochs with SGD with momentum set to 0.9, a
batch size of 10, and a learning rate of 10−6 and
0.003, respectively.

U-LiRA details. Instantiating U-LiRA on a large
language model is extremely computationally expensive due to the requirement of training a sufficient number of shadow models to properly estimate the forget and out distributions. However,
because our fine-tuning dataset is relatively small,
we can run U-LiRA in this setting, where each
shadow model is initialized from the pre-trained
model. We fine-tune the pre-trained model with
either only LIMA or LIMA and the random, PII,
and poison datasets; we repeat this procedure 20
times, with different data orderings, to produce
20 models where samples from random, PII, and
poison datasets are in training and 20 models
when they are not in training. For the 20 models
that were fine-tuned with the random, PII, and
poison datasets, we select one of the subsets to unlearn, creating 20 unlearned models, unlearned
with either GradDesc or NegGrad. In total this
created 20 models (fine-tuned on LIMA without
random, PII, and poison datasets) × 20 models
(fine-tuned on LIMA with random, PII, and poison
datasets and then one dataset is unlearned) × 3
(we unlearn each of the random, PII, and poison
datasets independently) × 2 (we run unlearning
with GradDesc or NegGrad) = 2, 400 models.
We then assign 10 models fine-tuned only on
LIMA, and 10 unlearned models as shadow models, and compute in and out shadow distributions
for each of the 100 samples from the forget set by
computing the loss on each example. We assign
the remaining 10 models fine-tuned only with
LIMA and 10 unlearned models as target models.
For each model, we record the prediction made
by U-LiRA across all 100 forget samples. We accumulate these predictions across all 20 target
models, and report overall attack accuracy. We
also implement the baseline U-MIA, training a
logistic regression model on the losses of a subset
of forget and test set samples.

6. Why does inexact unlearning provide little protection against ULiRA?
It is clear from Figure 4 that successful unlearning is heavily dependent on the step at which
unlearning is terminated. To successfully protect
against U-LiRA, the in shadow distribution – predictions of a sample from models that included
that sample in the forget set – should be identical
to the out shadow distribution. In order to have a
small attack accuracy, the unlearning algorithm
must ensure that every example in the forget set
has shadow in and out distributions that are near
identical.
Undermining this goal are sources of variance
when implementing an unlearning algorithm.
Firstly, there is inherent variance due randomness introduced by the unlearning procedure. All
inexact unlearning algorithms investigated in this
work use mini-batch gradient-based optimizers.
If we run the same unlearning algorithm twice
with different random seeds, the final loss of an
unlearned sample will be different between the
two models [34]. Similarly, two models trained
without a sample will have different final losses
on that sample. A sample may be vulnerable to

membership inference after unlearning simply
because of this variance – a sample could lie on
the tail of the in or out distributions and be easily
identifiable. The extent to the overlap between
the in and out shadow distributions indicate how
often we can expect to successful unlearning a
specific sample. Secondly, as we have already
seen, different samples are unlearned at different rates. Many of the unlearning algorithms
perform better when the forget set is large (see
results in Kurmanji et al. [24]). These unlearning
algorithms calibrate for average-case unlearning
success across all samples in the forget set, along
with the other criteria detailed in Section 2.1.
For example, SCRUB stops when the accuracy
of the forget set is close to that of a validation
set from the same distribution. However, U-LiRA
is not an average-case attack; decision rules are
constructed for each individual example in the
forget set. This mismatch creates a tension in
desiderata between the attack and defense; an
unlearning algorithm must balance all the criteria enumerated in Section 2.1, while U-LiRA is
only concerned with exploiting non-adherence to
the second criteria: the unlearned model, 𝜃 𝑓 , and
retrain-from-scratch model, 𝜃𝑟 , should be similar.
We analyze these two issues here.

loss when it wasn’t in training compared to the
loss when it is unlearned, is so large, membership
inference becomes trivial.

included in the mini-batch. At termination, ULiRA accuracy was still over 75%. While this is

by no means a successful unlearning algorithm.

7. Discussion
Inexact unlearning needs to be calibrated
to individual samples. In this paper, we find
that inexact unlearning schemes fail against perexample adversaries such as U-LiRA. Although it
may seem that inexact methods generally do not
work, we argue that inexact schemes covered in
this paper can work, but require extreme precision in their application. Namely, we observe that
often vulnerability against a privacy adversary
stems from an imprecise unlearning schedule –
it is hard to pinpoint when to terminate the unlearning process for a given sample (c.f. Figure 7).
Informally, we observe (from Figure 4) that samples become less vulnerable in the early stages of
unlearning. Yet as unlearning progresses, samples become more vulnerable once again. That

8. Conclusion
In this paper we demonstrated that inexact unlearning literature has thus far been overestimating performance against a privacy adversary. That
is because most prior work evaluated their performance using population MIAs, leading them
to conclude incorrectly that the failure of the adversary was due to a good unlearning scheme. By
launching a per-example MIA with the U-LiRA
adversary, we showed that the true membership
leakage remains high after unlearning.
Our work highlights the importance of using appropriate adversaries to evaluate systems that
are expected to work with sensitive data, since
weak attacks give a false sense of privacy. Yet,
we also demonstrate that in practice it is not at
all easy to choose such an adversary, even perexample MIA adversaries which use parametric
tests can fail if the initial parametric assumptions
are wrong. Similarly to the adversarial robustness literature [4], we argue that future unlearning schemes should precisely define what threat

model they consider and in doing that, explicitly
state the guarantees they need and expect – this
would in turn dictate which adversaries should
be considered.

B. More background on CF-k, EU-k and SCRUB unlearning algorithms

Here we expand on the brief discussions in Section 4.1 surrounding of CF-k, EU-k and SCRUB.
CF-k & EU-k. Goel et al. [10] propose two algorithms to unlearn; Catastrophic Forgetting-k (CF-k)
freezes the first 𝑘 layers of the network and fine-tunes the remaining layers on 𝐷𝑟 , whilst Exact
Unlearning-k (EU-k) re-initializes the unfrozen layers and retrains them on 𝐷𝑟 . The unfrozen layers
are the 𝑘 closest to the output. Despite its name, EU-k is an inexact unlearning approach unless all
layers are re-initialized, as there is no guarantee that the frozen layers will not retain any memorized
information about the forget set. Many of the top entries from the NeurIPS 2023 competition on
machine unlearning were based on variations of CF-k and EU-k [39].
SCRUB. Kurmanji et al. [24] introduce the SCRUB algorithm for unlearning. It optimizes two loss
terms to update the unlearned model. First, the KL divergence between the predictions the original
and unlearned models make on the forget set 𝐷 𝑓 . This term is maximized; the motivation behind this
is that the original model will likely perform well on forget set samples and so we want to increase
the error on these samples. Second, to preserve the accuracy on the retain set 𝐷𝑟 , the KL divergence
between the predictions on 𝐷𝑟 from the original model and the unlearned model are minimized (and
the cross-entropy loss on the unlearned model is also minimized). These two steps are alternated,
where the maximization step is usually stopped earlier than the minimization step.
One important contribution of SCRUB is deciding when to stop unlearning; the longer unlearning
proceeds for, the larger the error on the forget set is. A large forget set error is not always an ideal
outcome of unlearning, as we are trying to attain the level of performance on the forget set in
22

Inexact Unlearning Needs More Careful Evaluations to Avoid a False Sense of Privacy

comparison to a model that never saw the forget set in training. If the forget set comes from the same
distribution as retain set, the retrain-from-scratch model, 𝜃𝑟 , may still perform well on the forget
set. SCRUB deals with this by sampling a validation set from the same distribution as the forget set
and measuring error on this set after unlearning. SCRUB then selects the checkpoint throughout the
unlearning procedure where the forget set error is closest to the validation error. Note, this additional
“(R)ewinding” yields the SCRUB+R approach [24].

So far, our membership attack has focused on the binary hypothesis setting where an adversary
attempts to infer if an input was a member of the training set (in the forget set, or in the retain set
in Section 4.4) or not a member of the training set. In reality, an example could belong to one of
three sets: the forget set, the test set, or the retain set. We adapt U-LiRA to this three-way hypothesis
setting by constructing three shadow distributions for each sample; one for when the sample was in
the forget set, one for when the sample was in the retain set, and one for when the sample wasn’t
in the training set. Given a target model and a sample, we then measure the likelihood that this
sample belonged to the retain, forget, and test set, and assign membership to the set with the highest
likelihood. Importantly, if an example was in the forget set (or retain set) and is predicted as in the
retain set (or forget set), we determine this to be a failure, even though the example is still predicted
as in training. Under this new setting, we observed no decrease in membership inference accuracy, as
the inputs belonging to the retain set are easily identifiable – most errors come from misidentifying
an input in the forget set as a test input, and vice versa. To illustrate, we plot the shadow distributions
before and after unlearning for a specific CIFAR-10 example in Figure 11. Before unlearning (with
SCRUB), as expected, the shadow forget and retain distributions are identical, and there is little
overlap with the non-member (out distribution). After unlearning, the forget and out distributions
24

overlap and so these two cases are confused more often. However, the retain distribution is much
more separable, and so the ability to infer if an example was in the retain set, and not in the out or
forget set remains high. In general, membership inference after unlearning does not become more
difficult with the inclusion of the retain set distribution in the hypothesis testing problem.
